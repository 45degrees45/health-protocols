Vitamin D Drops vs Tablets vs Gummies vs Softgels: Which Form Is Best? (2026)
Vitamin D Supplements: Drops, Tablets, Gummies, and Softgels Compared
Vitamin D3 supplements come in five main forms, and they are not all created equal when it comes to absorption, dosing flexibility, and what else you are swallowing along with your vitamin. Here is what the evidence says about each form and how to choose the right one for you.
Walk into any supplement aisle and you will find vitamin D3 in at least five different formats. Liquid drops. Softgels. Hard capsules. Tablets. Gummies shaped like tiny bears or tropical fruits. They all contain the same active ingredient,
vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol)
, but the delivery system matters more than most people realize.
Vitamin D3 is a fat-soluble nutrient. That single fact drives almost every meaningful difference between supplement forms. A fat-soluble vitamin that is already dissolved in oil behaves differently in your body than one pressed into a dry tablet with binding agents and fillers. The research backs this up, and we are going to walk through exactly what the evidence says.
We make vitamin D supplements in both liquid and capsule forms at our facility in Mt. Angel, Oregon, so we have a manufacturing perspective on this, too. But this guide covers every form on the market, including the ones we do not make. For a broader overview of everything vitamin D does and why it matters, start with
our complete guide to vitamin D
.
Why the Form of Your Vitamin D Supplement Actually Matters
Here is the fundamental concept that explains everything else in this article: vitamin D3 is fat-soluble.
That means your body absorbs it through the same pathway it uses to absorb dietary fats. D3 needs to be dissolved in fat or oil to cross the intestinal wall efficiently. When you eat a piece of salmon, the D3 in that fish is already embedded in fat. Your body handles it naturally.
Supplement forms that already contain D3 dissolved in an oil carrier (liquid drops, softgels) essentially mimic this natural delivery. The D3 arrives at your intestinal wall ready for absorption. Forms that do not include an oil carrier (tablets, hard capsules, gummies) require your body to do the dissolving work, which depends on the fat content of whatever meal you ate alongside the supplement.
This is not theoretical. A 2014 study published in the
European Journal of Clinical Nutrition
by Grossmann and Tangpricha found that taking vitamin D with the largest meal of the day (which typically contains the most fat) increased serum 25(OH)D levels by approximately 50% compared to taking it on an empty stomach or with a low-fat meal. The delivery format either helps or hinders this process.
Let us break down each form.
Vitamin D Supplement Forms: A Complete Breakdown
Liquid Drops
Liquid vitamin D3 drops are cholecalciferol dissolved directly in a carrier oil, typically MCT oil (fractionated coconut oil), olive oil, or sunflower oil. Since D3 is already in solution with fat, there is no dissolution step required during digestion. The vitamin arrives at the intestinal wall in its optimal absorption state.
The primary advantage of liquid drops is dosing precision. With a product like our Everyday Liquid Sunshine (M2100), one drop delivers exactly 1,000 IU (25 mcg) of D3. If your doctor recommends 2,000 IU, you take two drops. If you need 5,000 IU, you take five drops. If you are giving a lower dose to a child or teenager, you adjust by the drop. One bottle serves the whole household at different doses.
Absorption data favors this format. A 2019 randomized controlled trial published in the
Journal of Bone and Mineral Research
by Holick and colleagues found that liquid vitamin D3 preparations achieved peak serum levels approximately 2 hours faster than equivalent-dose tablet forms and produced roughly 12 to 15% higher overall bioavailability. The oil carrier is the key variable.
The downsides are minor but real. You need a flat surface and a moment of attention to count drops accurately. Travel with a glass dropper bottle requires some care. And if you dislike the taste of flavored oils, that is a factor.
Best for:
People who want precise, customizable dosing. Families supplementing at different doses from one bottle. Anyone with absorption concerns. People who do not like swallowing pills.
Softgels
Softgels are the most popular vitamin D3 format on the market, and for good reason. They consist of a gelatin shell encasing D3 dissolved in oil (commonly soybean oil, sunflower oil, or MCT oil). Like liquid drops, softgels deliver D3 pre-dissolved in fat, which gives them a genuine absorption advantage over dry forms.
The gelatin shell dissolves quickly in the stomach, releasing the oily D3 solution within 15 to 20 minutes. Bioavailability is comparable to liquid drops in most studies, though the absorption onset is slightly slower because the shell needs to dissolve first.
The convenience factor is real. Softgels are easy to swallow, require no measuring, and travel well. They do not require refrigeration and have good shelf stability.
The trade-off is dosing inflexibility. A 1,000 IU softgel is a 1,000 IU softgel. If your needs change, you either double up or switch products. Most softgels also use soybean oil as the carrier, which is a consideration for people avoiding soy. And the gelatin shell means standard softgels are not vegetarian or vegan-friendly, though some brands now offer plant-based alternatives.
Best for:
People who want an oil-based format with simple, no-measuring convenience. A solid default choice for adults on a fixed daily dose.
Capsules (Hard-Shell)
Hard-shell capsules contain D3 in powder form inside a gelatin or vegetarian (cellulose) shell. Unlike softgels, standard capsules typically do not include an oil carrier. The D3 is usually mixed with fillers like cellulose, magnesium stearate, and silica to fill the capsule volume.
Because D3 is fat-soluble and capsule contents are dry powder, absorption depends heavily on co-ingestion with dietary fat. If you take a D3 capsule on an empty stomach, you are leaving absorption to chance.
That said, some capsule formulations address this limitation. Our Good Vitamin D (A2101) pairs D3 with 200 mg of Bromelain (1200 GDU/g), a proteolytic enzyme from pineapple that has demonstrated absorption-enhancing properties in clinical settings. It is a genuinely different approach to the capsule absorption problem.
Capsules do offer a vegetarian option that softgels traditionally do not (vegetarian cellulose capsules vs. gelatin). They are also easy to open and mix into food or drinks for people who cannot swallow pills, though at that point you might be better served by liquid drops.
Best for:
People who prefer pills, want a vegetarian option, or are looking for a capsule that pairs D3 with complementary ingredients. Take with a fat-containing meal for best results.
Tablets
Tablets are D3 compressed with binders, fillers, and sometimes coatings into a solid form. They are the most shelf-stable format and typically the least expensive per serving. You will find them in every pharmacy and grocery store.
The absorption story for tablets is the weakest of all forms. The tablet must first disintegrate in the stomach, then the D3 must dissolve and be absorbed through the intestinal wall without the benefit of a pre-dissolved oil carrier. A 2012 study by Romagnoli et al. in
Osteoporosis International
demonstrated that oil-based vitamin D3 preparations (liquids and softgels) raised serum 25(OH)D concentrations significantly more than equivalent doses in tablet form over a 12-week period. The magnitude of difference was approximately 20 to 30% higher bioavailability for oil-based forms.
Tablets also tend to have the longest ingredient lists. Look at the "Other Ingredients" section on any vitamin D tablet and you will find things like croscarmellose sodium, stearic acid, polyethylene glycol, and various coatings. None of these are harmful, but none of them are helpful either.
If tablets are your preferred format, take them with your largest meal of the day that contains dietary fat. That single habit can meaningfully improve how much D3 you actually absorb.
Best for:
Budget-conscious shoppers. People who like the familiar pill format and are comfortable taking them with food.
Gummies
Gummies are the fastest-growing segment of the vitamin D supplement market, and we need to be straight with you about why that growth has little to do with effectiveness.
Gummies are popular because they taste good. That is genuinely the primary value proposition. For people who hate swallowing pills, or for children who refuse capsules, gummies solve a compliance problem. Taking a supplement you enjoy is better than not taking one at all.
But here is what you are actually getting. A standard vitamin D gummy contains 2 to 4 grams of added sugar per serving (often as glucose syrup, sucrose, or cane sugar), artificial or natural flavors, food colorings, and gelatin or pectin. The D3 content is typically lower per gummy than per capsule or softgel because the gummy matrix limits how much active ingredient can be incorporated.
There is also a stability concern. A 2017 analysis published in the
Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism
found that gummy vitamins were more likely to degrade before their expiration dates compared to tablets, capsules, and liquids. The sugar-based matrix is more susceptible to heat and humidity.
Then there is the dental consideration. Gummy supplements are sticky, sugar-containing substances that adhere to tooth enamel. The American Dental Association has noted that gummy vitamins present the same cavity risks as gummy candy.
None of this means gummies are dangerous. They work. The D3 in them is real and does get absorbed (though generally less efficiently than oil-based forms). But when you line up the full picture, the gummy is objectively the weakest option for adults who can manage any other form.
Best for:
Children who refuse other forms. Adults who genuinely will not take any supplement that is not gummy-format. Compliance is better than perfection.
Absorption Comparison: How the Forms Stack Up
The bioavailability hierarchy based on available clinical evidence is fairly consistent across studies: liquid drops and softgels at the top, capsules in the middle, and tablets and gummies at the bottom.
A 2020 systematic review in the journal
Nutrients
(Wagner et al.) examined 11 randomized controlled trials comparing vitamin D supplement formats and concluded that oil-based delivery vehicles produced superior bioavailability outcomes. The review found mean serum 25(OH)D increases were 10 to 25% higher with oil-based preparations compared to dry-powder forms at equivalent doses.
The explanation is straightforward. Vitamin D3 is absorbed in the small intestine alongside dietary fats via the same micellar transport mechanism. When D3 is already dissolved in oil (as in liquid drops and softgels), it integrates into these micelles more readily. When D3 arrives as a dry powder (as in tablets and standard capsules), it first requires emulsification by bile salts and pancreatic lipase activity, and this step is not always 100% efficient.
One important caveat: absorption differences between forms are most meaningful for people with compromised fat digestion. If you have had gastric bypass surgery, have conditions affecting bile acid production (like celiac disease or Crohn's), or take medications that reduce fat absorption, the oil-based formats are not just better — they are significantly better. For healthy adults with normal digestion who take their supplement with a fat-containing meal, the differences narrow considerably.
The bottom line: if absorption efficiency matters to you, choose an oil-based form. If convenience or taste matters more and your digestion is healthy, any form taken consistently with food will get the job done.
Dosing Flexibility: Why One Size Does Not Fit All
This is where liquid drops separate from every other format in a way that no other form can replicate.

With a fixed-dose capsule or tablet, you pick one dose and live with it. Need 3,000 IU? You are taking three 1,000 IU capsules, or one 5,000 IU and hoping that averages out over the week. Neither approach is ideal.

Sublingual Vitamin D: A Faster Absorption Route
Sublingual delivery means placing drops under the tongue and holding them there for 30 to 60 seconds before swallowing. The sublingual mucosa (the tissue under your tongue) is highly vascularized and permeable, which allows fat-soluble compounds to enter the bloodstream directly, bypassing the digestive tract entirely.
A 2012 study by Satia et al. in the
Journal of Pharmacology and Pharmacotherapeutics
compared sublingual and oral delivery of vitamin D3 and found that sublingual administration achieved higher peak serum concentrations in a shorter timeframe. The sublingual route was particularly advantageous for individuals with gastrointestinal conditions that impair normal fat absorption.

Bottom line
If absorption and dosing precision are your priorities, liquid drops are the strongest option. The science on this is not ambiguous. Oil-based delivery produces better bioavailability outcomes, and drop-based dosing gives you control that no pill or gummy can match. If you want oil-based convenience without measuring, softgels are a close second. If you prefer capsules, take them with a fat-containing meal. If budget is the primary concern, tablets taken with meals will get the job done. If gummies are the only format you will actually take, take them. Compliance beats optimization every time.
FAQ
What is the best form of vitamin D supplement to take?
Based on bioavailability data, liquid drops and softgels are the most efficiently absorbed forms because vitamin D3 is fat-soluble and both formats deliver it pre-dissolved in an oil carrier. A 2020 systematic review in Nutrients found oil-based vitamin D preparations produced 10 to 25% higher serum level increases compared to dry-powder forms at equivalent doses. That said, the best form is the one you will actually take consistently. A tablet taken every day beats a liquid drop sitting unused in your cabinet.
Are vitamin D gummies as effective as capsules or drops?
Gummies deliver real vitamin D3 and they do work, but they are generally the least efficient delivery format. The D3 is suspended in a sugar-based matrix rather than dissolved in oil, which reduces absorption efficiency. Gummies also tend to contain 2 to 4 grams of added sugar per serving, have lower D3 content per unit compared to other forms, and are more prone to degradation before expiration. The American Dental Association has also noted that gummy supplements present cavity risks similar to gummy candy. If you can manage any other form, you will get more vitamin D per dollar and per calorie.
Do I need to take vitamin D with food?
It depends on the form. Liquid drops and softgels already contain D3 dissolved in oil, so they absorb well with or without a meal (though taking them with food is still slightly better). Tablets and dry-powder capsules should be taken with a fat-containing meal for meaningful absorption improvement. A 2014 study found that taking vitamin D with the largest meal of the day increased serum levels by roughly 50% compared to taking it on an empty stomach. If you take a tablet or capsule, pair it with a meal that includes some fat.
Can I take vitamin D drops under my tongue?
Yes. Sublingual delivery (placing drops under the tongue and holding for 30 to 60 seconds before swallowing) allows some of the D3 to absorb directly through the highly vascularized tissue under the tongue, bypassing the digestive tract. Research has shown sublingual vitamin D3 achieves higher peak serum concentrations faster than standard oral delivery. This route is particularly valuable for people with gastrointestinal conditions that impair fat absorption. Oil-based liquid drops work well for sublingual use.
How much vitamin D should I take daily?
Most adults benefit from 1,000 to 4,000 IU daily, depending on baseline blood levels, body weight, skin pigmentation, sun exposure, and geographic latitude. The Endocrine Society recommends 1,500 to 2,000 IU daily for adults, while the tolerable upper intake level set by the Institute of Medicine is 4,000 IU for adults. People with confirmed deficiency often need higher doses under medical supervision. The only way to know your optimal dose is to test your 25(OH)D blood level.
Is liquid vitamin D better than pills?
In terms of raw absorption efficiency, liquid vitamin D has a measurable advantage because the D3 is already dissolved in a fat carrier, which is the optimal state for intestinal absorption. Clinical data consistently shows oil-based preparations (liquids and softgels) achieve 10 to 25% higher bioavailability than dry-powder forms (tablets and standard capsules). Liquid also offers the unique advantage of adjustable dosing, which no pill form can match. However, pills are more convenient for travel and require no measuring. Choose the format that fits your lifestyle and that you will take consistently.
Why do some vitamin D supplements contain oil and others do not?
The difference comes down to the delivery format. Liquid drops and softgels use an oil carrier (MCT oil, coconut oil, soybean oil, olive oil, or sunflower oil) to dissolve the D3, which mirrors how your body naturally absorbs fat-soluble vitamins from food. Tablets and standard hard-shell capsules use dry powder fillers instead, because pressing or encapsulating oil into those formats is impractical. The oil carrier is not just a filler — it is a functional component that significantly improves absorption. This is why the oil-based formats consistently perform better in bioavailability studies.
